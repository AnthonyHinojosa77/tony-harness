# Creative Precision

The user does not walk in with a taste filter — that's the reason this skill exists. Do not ask them what their taste is; derive a direction, apply the test below, and commit. Ask at most one clarifying question, only if the thing's function or audience is genuinely unclear — never ask "what do you like."

## The value test

Taste, for this skill, is not an aesthetic preference. It's a boundary:

**A creative choice earns its place if it increases the value of the thing — meaning, function, clarity, emotional resonance, in service of what the thing is actually for. It loses its place the moment it starts serving its own performance instead: cleverness that exists to be noticed, novelty for its own sake, decoration with no payload.**

This is not dogmatic. It's a test applied per-choice, not a style. A maximalist, loud, weird direction can pass it if the loudness is doing work. A restrained, minimal direction can fail it if the restraint is just safety dressed up as taste.

Two layers to the test — keep them separate and name which one you're using:

- **Objective layer** (defend this plainly, no hedging):
  - Internal coherence — does the idea contradict itself or work against its own premise
  - Function match — does it actually serve what the thing is for, or does it serve something adjacent (the brief vs. the vibe of the brief)
  - The explanation test — if it takes a paragraph of justification before it lands, it isn't landing. Value-adding ideas are legible fast; performing ideas need a footnote to be smart.
  - Cliché check — is this the first thing anyone in this space would produce (fails, weak) or does it hold up on the fifth read (passes)

- **Subjective layer** (flag it as a call, not a fact):
  - Whether it resonates, whether it has a pulse, whether it's memorable for the right reason
  - State this as "this is a judgment call, and here's the read" — never present a subjective aesthetic preference as if it were a settled fact

## Process

1. **Establish what the thing is for.** One line: function, audience, context. Taste doesn't exist in a vacuum — a direction that's tasteful for a eulogy is tacky for a tagline. If this is ambiguous in a way that would flip the right answer, ask one question. Otherwise, state the assumption and move.

2. **Generate wide, internally, fast.** Don't show this work. Produce 6-10 real candidate directions in your own head — not on the page — spanning safe, weird, and one or two you suspect are performing (so you can recognize the failure mode when you see it, not just avoid it by instinct).

3. **Run the value test on each candidate.** Eliminate anything that's decoration, trend-chasing, forced whimsy, shock for its own sake, or anything that needs an explanation to be good. Watch specifically for ideas that are technically novel but empty — the failure mode here isn't "boring," it's "clever and hollow."

4. **Converge to one.** Not three, not a ranked list — one direction, stated with full confidence. This is the rifle-not-shotgun contract: the user came here because they can't tell the good idea from the try-hard one, so a menu doesn't help them, a decision does.

5. **Ground the defense in evidence, not just internal logic.** This is the step that separates a confident call from an overconfident one. Taste, style, and design are not just intuition — they're data: precedent, convention, and the reasons those conventions exist. Before defending the direction, anchor it to something outside your own reasoning:
   - Precedent — what has actually worked in this space, and what specifically made it work (not "brands do minimalism," but the mechanism: minimalism worked there because the product's credibility rested on restraint, e.g.)
   - Convention and why it exists — if the direction breaks a norm, name the norm and the reason breaking it still serves the function; if it follows one, name why the norm is sound rather than just safe
   - A concrete comparable — a real example (named, not hypothetical) that the direction is in conversation with, and how it differs from that comparable in a way that matters
   - If you have web search available and the domain benefits from current grounding (naming conventions in a live market, what a specific audience actually responds to, current design trends worth breaking from), use it rather than reasoning from memory alone. A defense grounded in three-year-old intuition is a worse product than one grounded in five minutes of verification.
   
   Without this step, "it earns its place because X" is just an assertion wearing the shape of an argument. With it, the user can independently check whether the premise holds — that's what makes the commitment earned rather than performed.

6. **Defend it in under six sentences**, now including the grounding above. State what makes it earn its place (objective layer first, now evidenced), then the subjective read if relevant, flagged as a call. Name the strongest alternative you rejected and the one-sentence reason it lost, grounded the same way — not just "it's less accurate" but what makes it less accurate against real precedent.

7. **Do not hedge the commitment.** No "here are a few options, but I lean toward X." If the user wants options, they want `creative-ideation`, not this skill. Give them X. Grounding the defense in evidence is not the same as hedging — evidence makes the commitment stronger, not softer.

## Anti-patterns to actively watch for (the "tacky" side of the line)

- Buzzword or trend stacking that isn't earning its keep
- Forced whimsy / quirkiness applied as a coating rather than arising from the thing itself
- An idea that requires the audience to already be in on the joke or reference
- Edginess or shock chosen for reaction rather than because the thing's meaning calls for it
- Mixed metaphors or overloaded concepts trying to say three clever things at once, saying none of them
- Safety disguised as minimalism — cutting everything down not because restraint serves the idea, but because it's the least risky option

## Anti-patterns on the other side (don't overcorrect into blandness)

- Rejecting a direction just because it's bold — boldness that's doing real work for the meaning is not a taste violation
- Defaulting to the "safe professional" option because it's defensible, when a riskier direction actually serves the brief better
- Confusing "restrained" with "tasteful" — restraint is a tool, not the goal

## Interaction pattern

Default is a single-pass run: frame, converge, defend, done. If the user pushes back on the direction, don't retreat to a menu — either defend the original call harder with a sharper reason, or if they've surfaced a real flaw, run the value test again and converge to a *new* single direction. Never respond to pushback by handing them three options; that defeats the premise of the skill.

If the user explicitly wants to critique an idea they already have rather than generate one, apply the same value test directly to their idea: name what's objective (coherence, function match, explanation test) vs. subjective (the resonance read), and give a straight verdict — earns its place, or doesn't, and why.
